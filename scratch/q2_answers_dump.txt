Question: Explain why desegregation in education faced severe resistance in the years 1954–57. (12 marks)
Answer:
<strong>Model Answer Blueprint:</strong>

Paragraph 1 (Political Resistance): Southern politicians organized systematic resistance. Governors like Faubus of Arkansas used state institutions, such as the National Guard at Little Rock, to directly defy federal integration orders, validating local white opposition and creating legal blockades.

Paragraph 2 (Social Backlash): Grassroots segregationists formed White Citizens' Councils and KKK groups to terrorize Black families. Mob violence at school gates, like that faced by the Little Rock Nine, made integration physically dangerous and socially isolating.

Paragraph 3 (Federal Conflict): Eisenhower's reluctant intervention and the subsequent closure of Little Rock high schools in 1958 proved that Southern authorities preferred shutting down education over desegregating public schools.

Question: Explain why civil rights legislation was passed in the years 1964–65. (12 marks)
Answer:
<strong>Model Answer Blueprint:</strong>

Paragraph 1 (The Power of Media): Non-violent direct action campaigns in Birmingham (1963) and Selma (1965) provoked violent responses from Southern authorities (Bull Connor's dogs, Bloody Sunday troopers). This televised brutality shocked the American public, creating a moral crisis that demanded federal legislative action.

Paragraph 2 (Federal Leadership): President Johnson used the national grief after the assassination of John F. Kennedy to push the Civil Rights Act of 1964 through Congress as a tribute to Kennedy, successfully overcoming a record-breaking Southern Dixiecrat filibuster.

Paragraph 3 (Voting Pressure): The Selma campaign highlighted that local registrar systems were completely shut to Black citizens. This forced the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which banned literacy tests and sent federal registrars to register voters in the South.

--- subtopic subtopic_3_2 ---
Question: Explain why US military involvement in Vietnam escalated in the years 1964–68. (12 marks)
Answer:
<strong>Model Answer Blueprint:</strong>

Paragraph 1 (The Trigger): The Gulf of Tonkin Incident in August 1964 provided the political trigger. Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which served as a 'blank check' giving President Johnson the authority to deploy combat troops without a formal declaration of war.

Paragraph 2 (Containment Policy): The Cold War containment policy and Domino Theory drove military planners. US leaders believed that if South Vietnam fell, neighboring nations would collapse like dominoes, making containment a vital national security priority.

Paragraph 3 (The Attrition Strategy): General Westmoreland's strategy of attrition required massive troop increases. The US launched Operation Rolling Thunder in early 1965 and initiated 'Search and Destroy' tactics, requiring troop levels to surge to over 500,000 by 1968 to find and destroy Vietcong forces.

Answer:
<strong>Model Answer Blueprint:</strong>

Paragraph 1 (Patriotism and Anti-Communism): Many Americans, particularly the conservative working class, held deep-seated anti-communist beliefs. They believed that abandoning South Vietnam would lead to a communist bloodbath and destroy US global credibility, endorsing Nixon's 'Domino Theory' defense.

Paragraph 2 (Nixon's Political Mobilization): President Nixon actively mobilized these voters. His famous 'Silent Majority' speech in November 1969 gave a voice to patriotic, law-abiding citizens, contrasting them with radical student protesters and boosting public support for his policies.

Paragraph 3 (Class Resentment): There was severe class resentment among the working class toward middle-class student protesters. Working-class families, whose sons did not have college deferments and were drafted, viewed student demonstrators as privileged, unpatriotic draft-dodgers, leading to pro-war events like the 'Hard Hat Riots' of 1970.

Question: Explain why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s.
Answer:
One reason why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s was that federal judicial rulings provided constitutional backing for discriminatory practices. In 1896, the landmark Supreme Court decision in Plessy v. Ferguson established the separate but equal doctrine. This legal precedent allowed Southern states to pass sweeping Jim Crow laws enforcing segregation in schools, public transport, and restaurants, making racial separation constitutionally permissible and systematically embedded. Therefore, federal judicial approval was the essential foundation of Southern segregation, because it legally protected discriminatory state laws from constitutional challenges.

A second reason why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s was that Southern state authorities systematically disenfranchised Black voters to preserve white political power. States introduced impossible literacy tests, requiring Black applicants to explain complex constitutional clauses, alongside poll taxes and grandfather clauses. This deliberate disenfranchisement meant that by 1956, only 20% of eligible Southern Black citizens were registered to vote, leaving white segregationist politicians unchallenged in state legislatures. Consequently, political disenfranchisement was a crucial cause of continued discrimination because it prevented Black citizens from obtaining the democratic representation needed to challenge racist local policies.

A third reason why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s was that segregation was ruthlessly enforced through organized white social resistance and violent vigilante terror. The Ku Klux Klan and White Citizens' Councils carried out lynchings, bombings, and economic intimidation, such as firing Black workers who attempted to register. This campaign of intimidation was reinforced by local police complicity and all-white juries who routinely refused to convict white attackers, creating a constant climate of fear. As a result, grassroots violence and state-level complicity were decisive causes of segregation because they made active resistance too dangerous, ensuring compliance on a daily basis.

Question: Explain why US military involvement in Vietnam escalated in the years 1964–68.
Answer:
One reason why US military involvement in Vietnam escalated in the years 1964–68 was that Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution following military clashes in August 1964. The US destroyer USS Maddox reported clashes with North Vietnamese patrol boats, leading Congress to grant President Johnson the authority to take all necessary measures to defend US interests, effectively serving as a blank check to wage war without a formal declaration. Consequently, this resolution was a vital political cause of escalation because it removed all legislative checks, allowing Johnson to order the deployment of the first 3,500 Marines to Da Nang in March 1965.

A second reason why US military involvement in Vietnam escalated in the years 1964–68 was that geopolitical fears of communist expansion drove the massive commitment of US troops and resources under the policy of containment. Under the Domino Theory, US advisors believed that if South Vietnam fell, neighboring nations like Laos and Cambodia would fall next. To prevent this, General Westmoreland implemented a strategy of attrition and search-and-destroy missions, causing US troop levels to surge to over 535,000 by 1968. Therefore, Cold War containment was the underlying strategic cause of escalation because it convinced US decision-makers that military victory in Vietnam was essential to stop global communist expansion.

A third reason why US military involvement in Vietnam escalated in the years 1964–68 was that the military responded to the strategic shock of the communist Tet Offensive in early 1968. In January 1968, the Vietcong launched a coordinated surprise attack on over 100 cities and towns in South Vietnam, including the US Embassy in Saigon. Although it was a military defeat for the Vietcong, the offensive shattered US claims of imminent victory, creating a massive credibility gap at home and forcing Westmoreland to request 206,000 more troops. As a result, the Tet Offensive was a critical turning point in the escalation because it proved that current military levels were failing, forcing the US administration to choose between open-ended escalation or negotiating an exit.

Question: Explain why the USA was concerned about the future of Vietnam in 1963.
Answer:
One reason why the USA was concerned about the future of Vietnam in 1963 was the geopolitical threat of communist expansion in Southeast Asia. Under the Domino Theory, President Kennedy's advisors believed that the fall of South Vietnam to communism would trigger the rapid collapse of neighboring countries like Laos, Cambodia, and Thailand. This containment doctrine framed South Vietnam as a vital Cold War bulwark that the US could not afford to lose. Therefore, the Domino Theory was the fundamental cause of US concern in 1963 because it elevated Vietnam's local conflict into a critical battleground for global containment.

A second reason why the USA was concerned about the future of Vietnam in 1963 was the political instability and corruption of Ngo Dinh Diem's South Vietnamese government. Diem, a Catholic, discriminated against the Buddhist majority, leading to mass protests and public self-immolations that shocked the international community. His regime ignored US advice, used brutal secret police tactics, and alienated the rural population, destroying South Vietnamese domestic support. Consequently, the failures of the Diem regime were a major cause of concern because they proved that the South Vietnamese state was collapse-prone from within, undermining US aid efforts.

A third reason why the USA was concerned about the future of Vietnam in 1963 was that the growing strength of the Vietcong communist insurgency created immediate fears of a South Vietnamese military defeat. The Vietcong successfully infiltrated rural areas, and Diem's unpopular Strategic Hamlet Program pushed angry peasants to support them. By 1963, the Vietcong were winning battles against the South Vietnamese Army, raising fears that Saigon would fall before the US could stabilize the country. As a result, the rapid rise of the Vietcong was a decisive cause of concern because it demonstrated that without direct US intervention, South Vietnam was on the verge of military defeat.

Question: Explain why US involvement in the Vietnam War changed under President Nixon.
Answer:
One reason why US involvement in the Vietnam War changed under President Nixon was the implementation of Vietnamisation, which aimed to withdraw US troops while transferring the combat burden. Under the Nixon Doctrine, Nixon began the systematic withdrawal of US ground troops, reducing forces from over 500,000 to under 25,000 by 1972, while training the South Vietnamese army. This policy was designed to appease domestic anti-war sentiment by reducing US casualties. Therefore, Vietnamisation was a primary cause of changed involvement because it marked the transition of the US role from active ground combat to an advisory and supportive capacity.

A second reason why US involvement in the Vietnam War changed under President Nixon was that intense domestic anti-war protests and political pressure on the home front forced a change in policy. Events like the exposure of the My Lai Massacre in 1969, the Kent State shootings in 1970, and the leaking of the Pentagon Papers in 1971 destroyed public trust in the presidency. The resulting credibility gap made it politically impossible for Nixon to maintain large-scale US ground involvement. Consequently, domestic political unrest was a decisive cause of changed involvement because it forced the administration to accelerate troop withdrawals to prevent a total political collapse at home.

A third reason why US involvement in the Vietnam War changed under President Nixon was that Nixon shifted the military focus toward aggressive air campaigns to destroy communist supply lines while ground involvement decreased. Nixon ordered the invasion of neutral Cambodia in 1970 and Laos in 1971, and launched Operation Linebacker bombings on Hanoi. These secret and public operations aimed to disrupt the Ho Chi Minh Trail and force North Vietnam to negotiate, protecting withdrawing US forces. As a result, this military shift was a key cause of changed involvement because it replaced American ground combat casualties with heavy air power and regional incursions, aiming to achieve peace with honor.

A third reason why opposition in the USA to the Vietnam War increased in the years 1964–73 was the unfairness of the [[conscription]] draft system and the impact of unedited television coverage. Question: Explain why the Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56) was successful.
Answer:
One reason why the Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56) was successful was that the Montgomery Improvement Association (MIA) provided exceptional organization and alternative transport. Formed in December 1955 and led by the young minister [[Martin Luther King Jr.]], the MIA organized a highly efficient [[carpool system]] using over 300 private cars and designated pickup stations. This allowed Black citizens to avoid using the buses while still getting to work daily, sustaining the protest for [[381 days]] despite city harassment. Consequently, the MIA's logistical efficiency prevented the boycott from collapsing due to practical difficulties, maintaining economic pressure on the bus company.

A second reason why the Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56) was successful was that the boycott achieved its ultimate success through a decisive legal victory in the federal courts. In November 1956, the US Supreme Court upheld the district court ruling in [[Browder v. Gayle]], which declared that segregation on public buses was unconstitutional under the 14th Amendment. This federal intervention overrode the local Montgomery city council's refusal to integrate, legally forcing the bus company to end segregation. Therefore, the Supreme Court's ruling was the crucial factor because it provided the legal authority needed to officially end the segregation policy, giving the boycott its concrete victory.

A third reason why the Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56) was successful was the unity of the Black community and the power of their economic pressure. Black citizens made up roughly [[70%]] of Montgomery's bus riders, and their near-total participation in the boycott bankrupt the bus company and damaged downtown white businesses. This solidarity was solidified by the respect for [[Rosa Parks]], whose arrest acted as a unifying test case, and by King's inspiring, non-violent leadership. As a direct result, this economic leverage and community resolve showed that peaceful mass action could disrupt segregated systems, forcing white authorities to recognize the strength of the civil rights movement.

This event showed how Black Power had captured the imagination of Black youth, providing them with a sense of dignity and international solidarity that traditional civil rights campaigns had failed to deliver in the late 1960s.

In response to the ruling, white Southerners formed [[White Citizens' Councils]] to use economic intimidation against civil rights activists.
